Goal

Give shoppers the ability to scan items themselves on the shop floor and pay without waiting at the checkout. When we started, similar flows already existed in global markets, so I could look at an established category of mobile self-scanning products. But for Russian Decathlon shoppers this was completely new behaviour. It created extra risk at first use and demanded careful attention to onboarding and to communicating the value of the service.

Reduce checkout congestion

Long queues at peak hours were one of the main sources of negative customer experience.

Speed up service

Less time at the till means higher throughput for the store.

Build infrastructure for self-checkout

The project was the first step toward a more autonomous purchase process.

Create a new customer experience

One of the first sports retailers in Russia to introduce this kind of purchase flow.

Market context

The market already knew the Scan & Go concept. Large international retailers, including Walmart and Sam's Club, had launched similar solutions. For Russian Decathlon shoppers it was still unfamiliar. The team's challenge was not to invent a new technology, but to make the existing approach so clear that a user could complete it without staff help or extra instructions.

The job was not to invent the technology. It was to make an existing one obvious.

Project constraints

The project was developed under significant system constraints.

  • The checkout system did not connect to the mobile app in real time
  • Prices could vary between locations, so the app had to identify the specific store
  • Some products used RFID, others only EAN barcodes, and each type carried its own logic
  • The mobile flow had to work even when GPS failed

MVP launch approach

The project was delivered within a constrained timeline and an evolving technical environment. To mitigate risk I focused on an intuitive onboarding flow, on simplifying the key user decisions, and on minimising errors during the critical first-use experience.

Decision matrix

The heaviest constraint shaped the heaviest decision. With no live link to the tills, the cart had to reach the cash register somehow.

Problem

No integration with the cash register (POS)

Everything downstream had to be designed around this one.

Full POS integration

  • Slow to build across 60+ stores

  • Fast to build
  • Low cost

  • The cashier stays in the loop

What was hard

The key question I brought to the Product Owner at the start was simple. Would people actually understand that they could shop straight from their phones while standing in front of a shelf? This was not an online store and not a traditional checkout. It introduced a shopping behaviour that customers had never met before.

Onboarding a new behaviour

The first screen after launch was an empty cart. If the next step was not obvious, the flow broke before the first scan.

What I didI treated the empty state as part of onboarding: cut the instructions, and made the scan button visually dominant so the start of the interaction was immediately clear.

Identifying the store

The app needed to know exactly which store the shopper was standing in, because prices could differ between locations.

What I didTwo routes into the flow: automatic detection via GPS, and a manual fallback that scans a QR code at the store entrance.

Two scanning types, one flow

RFID tags are unique per item, barcodes are not. That means different grouping rules in the cart, different data formats for the QR code, and different constraints when adding items.

What I didA single scanning flow that handles both types invisibly, with no technical explanations surfacing in the UI.

Payment without POS sync

The app did not sync with the checkout software in real time. It generated a QR code carrying the cart data, which a cashier then scanned manually.

What I didHid the technical limitation behind one clearly marked next step, "show the QR code at the till", with a code large enough to read at a distance.
