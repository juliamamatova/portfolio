Fieldman: the whole working day of a utility technician,
held in one glove-friendly app

A mobile field kit for meter technicians — the route, the work order, the clock and the truck stock in a single tool that keeps working when the signal doesn't.

Role
Product Designer
Platform
iOS & Desktop
Scope
Product architecture, flows, UI system
Users
Field crews, dispatch, supervisors

Goal

Give a field technician one app that holds the entire working day — clocking on, the route, every work order, the photo evidence and the truck inventory — and make it survive a basement with no signal. The same data feeds dispatch and payroll, so the app had to serve four audiences at once without ever asking the person in the field to think about any of them.

Field technician

Runs the route, completes meter reads and exchanges, captures photos and serials, and clocks their own hours.

Dispatch

Builds the day's routes, injects emergency jobs mid-shift, and needs to know where a crew is without calling them.

Crew supervisor

Approves timesheets, reviews rejected work orders, and tracks completion across a dozen technicians.

Back office

Receives the completed work orders as billable records — with the readings, serials and GPS stamp attached.

Paper in the truck, data a day late

A crew left the yard with a printed route sheet and a clipboard. Readings were written by hand, photographed on a personal phone and typed into the back office the next morning — by which time a wrong digit was untraceable. Hours were reconstructed from memory at the end of the week. Dispatch's only way to reach a technician mid-route was to call them while they were driving.

The constraint was never the interface. It was one hand, bright sun, and no bars of signal.

Key design challenges

A form nobody wants to fill in

A meter exchange is twelve required fields, and it happens twenty times a day. Split into four cards with a visible step counter, with the camera and scanner inside the fields they fill, so the technician types four things instead of twelve.

Offline as the normal case

Basements, crawl spaces and rural routes mean no signal is routine, not an error. Everything writes locally first and syncs behind the scenes; the only place the app admits it is a counter on the profile, so the queue is visible without ever blocking the work.

Readable in direct sun, usable in gloves

The interface is built on light grounds with strong contrast on the data itself, 44px minimum targets, and status carried by colour and shape together — so a dropped glove is never the reason a job goes unrecorded.

Time as a payroll record

The timesheet is not a tracker, it is evidence. Every block is named and timestamped as it happens, and the technician can add a missed lunch or standby block themselves rather than arguing about it a week later.

Inside the product

Six screens carry the technician's day: the clock-in, the route, the map, the work order, the timesheet and the truck. Every one of them is drawn for a phone held in a gloved hand, in daylight, on a network that comes and goes — a soft sky gradient for depth, one amber accent reserved for status, and machine data set in a mono face so serials and coordinates stay scannable.

Map — dispatchThe phone's map screen given room to work. The selected crew turns blue and draws its route, the cards carry the same glass tiles the technician reads on their own home screen, and the two actions a dispatcher takes — reroute, or hand over a job — are the only buttons on the page.

UI kit and components

The system is small on purpose. Two colours carry meaning — blue is something you tap, amber is something you should notice — and everything else is neutral. Two typefaces: a heavy geometric for numbers, a narrow humanist for machine data. Every component below is the live element lifted from the screens, not a redraw.
