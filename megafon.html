Goal

Create a mobile tool that replaces fragmented communication channels and gives every employee — from a sales associate to a commercial director — the right slice of data and tasks, directly on their phone. The app had to work for five types of users with different permissions, different levels of analytics depth and different sets of actions, and still feel like a single product.

Store employee

Receives tasks from their manager, marks completion, and tracks personal sales KPIs and salary accruals.

Territory Manager

Assigns tasks to stores in their zone, monitors completion, and tracks sales across their territory.

Regional Manager

Tracks plan execution across territory managers and stores, identifies deviations, and initiates corrective actions.

Head office

Monitors key sales metrics across the entire retail network without access to operational modules.

Hundreds of stores, no shared tool

Megafon's retail network in 2020 was hundreds of stores across Russia, thousands of employees and a complete absence of any unified management tool. Tasks came through WhatsApp and on paper. Sales data was only accessible from a PC. Absent staff were discovered after the fact.

The job was not to design five apps for five roles. It was to design one product that shows each of them a different face.

What was hard

A five-level role model

Each role sees a different set of data, actions and reporting lines — without confusing users or the team building it.

Analytics without overload

A Territory Manager sees aggregate sales across all their stores; Head Office sees the whole network. The depth had to be there without causing cognitive overload.

Tasks as a communication channel

A task isn't just a ticket. It's a chat, an event history, delegation and approval — all in one flow.

MVP without user testing

We shipped based on requirements, then collected analytics and interviews. The architecture had to be right the first time.

The visibility matrix

The first artefact of the project: who sees which module, and how deep. Keeping this in one table is what let us stay one product instead of several separate apps, and gave engineering a single source of truth for permissions.

Five decisions that shaped the product

  • 01 Role and flow decomposition

    A visibility matrix for five roles with different levels of access to screens and functionality. This approach let us keep a single product rather than building several separate apps.

  • 02 Task tracker architecture

    One flow for working with a task: from creation to archive, including incoming and outgoing tasks, delegation, deadline alignment and group scenarios.

  • 03 Hierarchical sales analytics

    A multi-level drill-down system that lets users move from summary metrics to details by date, metric and reporting structure.

  • 04 Comments and task events

    A chat model inside a task with mentions, replies to messages, and a clear separation between user communication and system events.

  • 05 Post-launch iteration

    An iterative improvement cycle based on product analytics and employee interviews, including analytical tools for new roles.

What analytics and interviews showed

The MVP shipped on requirements, without user testing. Everything below came out of the product analytics and employee interviews I ran in the first months of live use.

Tasks need completion verification

How I validated it

Interviews with Territory Managers two months after launch, plus an analysis of the share of tasks closed without a "done" status.

What it showed

Reading a task didn't guarantee it was actually done. Managers needed status control and a "task accepted" confirmation.

Table format overwhelms users

How I validated it

Scroll depth on the analytics screens, measured against the number of clicks into detailed tables versus the home screen.

What it showed

Users focused on summary numbers; detailed tables were rarely used. Visualisation would speed up comprehension.

Gamification doesn't explain progress

How I validated it

A round of interviews with store employees three months after launch.

What it showed

Users didn't understand how points and rankings were calculated, which reduced engagement with the motivation programme.

Chat needs @mentions

How I validated it

Interaction patterns inside tasks, and the share of messages addressed to one specific participant.

What it showed

The @mention let users pull the right person into a discussion much faster.

Hard to track task updates

How I validated it

The count of repeat task opens with no new user action afterwards.

What it showed

An update indicator on the task card was needed, so new activity could be found without opening every task.
